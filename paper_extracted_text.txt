• A statistical time-series feature classification and pixel-wise TCEV modeling method
was developed for SAR flood anomaly mapping.
• TFAMs provide continuous representations of flood anomalies and capture their

• Extreme-event characterization provides an interpretable strategy for SAR-based flood

Abstract
Flood mapping using Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) observations is essential for rapid
disaster response, yet existing approaches often struggle to characterize rare extreme flood
events due to limited temporal context and insufficient representation of flood anomalies.
This study proposes a statistical time-series feature classification and pixel-wise TCEV
modeling method for SAR flood anomaly mapping by integrating extreme value theory.
Long-term Sentinel-1 SAR time series were used to construct nine statistical features for
flood classification using a Tabular Prior-data Fitted Network (TabPFN), while a separately
fitted Two-Component Extreme Value (TCEV) model was used to calculate event-period
anomaly probabilities. The resulting spatial classification mask and TCEV-based anomaly
probabilities were then combined to produce Temporal Flood Anomaly Maps (TFAMs). The
proposed method was evaluated using four extreme flood events under diverse environ-
mental conditions. The generated TFAMs characterized flood-related temporal anomalies,
with the highest ROC-AUC 0.87 and average precision 0.81 achieved for New South Wales.
The corresponding TFAM-derived binary flood extent map achieved the highest F1-score
of 0.81, IoU of 0.69, and Kappa coefficient of 0.72.
Keywords: flood mapping; SAR time series; TCEV model; deep learning; TabPFN

1. Introduction
The increasing frequency and severity of natural disasters under global environmental
change have posed substantial challenges to disaster prevention and risk management [1,2].
According to the 2025 Global Assessment Report on Disaster Risk Reduction issued by the
United Nations Office for Disaster Risk Reduction, disaster risks are intensifying worldwide.
Among various natural hazards, floods are among the most frequent and destructive events,
causing severe impacts on human lives, infrastructure, and ecosystems [3]. Globally, floods
result in hundreds of billions of dollars in economic losses annually and remain a major
threat to sustainable development and societal resilience [ 4]. Therefore, accurate and
timely flood mapping is essential for emergency response, hazard assessment, and post-
disaster recovery.
Remote sensing technologies provide an effective approach for large-scale and rapid
flood monitoring. In particular, Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) has become one of the
most widely used data sources for flood mapping due to its all-weather and day-and-night
imaging capabilities [ 5–7]. The open-access Sentinel-1 SAR mission, with high spatial
resolution and frequent revisit capability, provides valuable observations for capturing
flood dynamics over large regions [8–10].
The fundamental principle of SAR-based flood mapping relies on the distinct electro-
magnetic response of water surfaces, which generally exhibit lower backscatter intensity
compared with surrounding land surfaces [11]. Existing SAR flood mapping approaches
can generally be divided into single-image-based and multi-temporal methods [12]. Single-
image approaches typically extract flooded areas from individual SAR acquisitions using
techniques such as thresholding, texture analysis, and region-growing segmentation [13,14].
Although computationally efficient, these methods are highly sensitive to environmental
conditions because certain land-cover types, including vegetated areas and wet snow, may
exhibit scattering characteristics similar to water surfaces, leading to false detections and
classification uncertainty [15,16]. To overcome these limitations, multi-temporal approaches
exploit backscatter variations between pre-flood and post-flood SAR observations to iden-
tify flood-induced changes [ 17]. However, their performance strongly depends on the
availability and quality of appropriate reference images. In regions with limited satellite
observations, irregular acquisition intervals, or complex environmental conditions, select-
ing suitable pre-flood baseline images remains challenging, which restricts their robustness
and operational applicability [18,19].
Recently, machine learning and deep learning approaches have demonstrated consid-
erable potential for improving SAR-based flood mapping [20,21]. Convolutional Neural
Networks (CNNs) and other deep architectures can automatically extract complex spatial
and temporal patterns from remote sensing observations, reducing the dependence on man-
ually designed features [22–24]. These data-driven approaches have achieved promising
performance in various flood mapping applications.
However, most existing deep learning models regard flood mapping primarily as a
pattern recognition problem and rely on the statistical characteristics of training samples.
Such data-driven approaches may suffer from limited generalization when applied to un-
seen environments or rare events, because the learned representations are influenced by the
distribution and diversity of training data [25]. Consequently, these models may perform
well under common flood conditions but remain less effective in identifying rare and ex-
treme flood events. In practice, floods represent abnormal deviations from normal surface
conditions rather than ordinary land-cover transitions [26]. Therefore, accurately character-
izing the degree of abnormality and rarity of SAR observations is crucial for improving the
robustness and interpretability of flood detection models [27–29]. This limitation indicates
that flood mapping requires not only learning discriminative features from data but also

incorporating knowledge about extreme event behavior. Integrating statistical or physical
knowledge with data-driven learning has been increasingly recognized as an effective
strategy for improving the reliability and interpretability of environmental models [ 30].
Developing a method that can combine temporal representation learning with statistically
interpretable extreme-event characterization remains an important challenge [31].
Extreme Value Theory (EVT) provides a statistical method for modeling rare events
and has been widely applied in hydrology, climatology, and risk analysis [32,33]. Unlike
conventional statistical methods that describe average conditions, EVT focuses on the tail
behavior of probability distributions and is therefore naturally suited for representing
extreme flood events. Among EVT-based approaches, the Two-Component Extreme Value
(TCEV) model is particularly attractive because it considers heterogeneous extreme mecha-
nisms by separating ordinary variations from extreme events [34]. For multi-temporal SAR
observations, TCEV enables the characterization of each pixel according to its historical
distribution and provides a quantitative measure of how unusual a given observation is
relative to its long-term behavior. Nevertheless, EVT-based approaches have rarely been
integrated with temporal deep learning methods for SAR flood mapping. Existing methods
typically use statistical models or deep learning independently, while the potential of
combining extreme-event theory with statistical feature construction from multi-temporal
SAR observations remains largely unexplored. Combining statistical time-series feature
classification with pixel-wise TCEV-based anomaly characterization may provide a useful
pathway for statistically interpretable SAR flood anomaly mapping.
In this study, we develop a framework that integrates statistical features constructed
from long-term Sentinel-1 observations, TabPFN (v2.6)-based spatial classification, and
pixel-wise TCEV modeling for SAR flood anomaly mapping. The nine statistical descriptors
C1–C9 are used by TabPFN to identify the event-informed maximum flood extent, whereas
the separately fitted TCEV distribution is used to characterize the statistical rarity of event-
period VH observations at each pixel. These two components are subsequently combined
to generate TFAMs that represent spatial differences in SAR anomaly scores across the
available acquisition dates. The main contributions of this study are as follows:
(1) We integrate two complementary components: TabPFN uses nine statistical descrip-
tors constructed from historical seasonal SAR observations to identify potential flood
areas, while a separately fitted TCEV model evaluates the statistical rarity of event-
period observations at each pixel. Their integration combines spatial classification
with historical anomaly assessment.
(2) We construct continuous Temporal Flood Anomaly Maps (TFAMs) that characterize
pixel-specific SAR anomalies within the estimated flood mask. These maps comple-
ment binary inundation delineation by representing spatial differences in anomaly
scores and their changes across available acquisition dates.
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces the study
areas and datasets. Section 3 describes SAR preprocessing, statistical feature construction,
TCEV fitting, TabPFN classification, TFAM generation, and accuracy assessment. Section 4
presents the flood mapping results, the supplementary hydro-meteorological consistency
assessment for Guangxi, and changes in SAR anomaly patterns between available acquisi-
tion dates for each event. Section 5 discusses extreme-event characterization, the integration
of statistical modeling and classification, and the interpretation of continuous anomaly
maps. Section 6 presents the conclusions and future research directions.

2. Study Area and Data
2.1. Study Area and Flood Events
To examine the applicability of the proposed TCEV-guided temporal learning method,
four extreme flood events were selected as case studies based on the availability of long-
term and event-period Sentinel-1 observations, documented flood occurrence, contrasting
environmental conditions, and available reference or auxiliary data (Figure 1a). Spanning
different continents and complex land-cover types, these areas provide contrasting settings
for an initial assessment of the method’s capability to capture low-frequency, high-impact
inundation anomalies against various background environments. A brief introduction to
each of these extreme flood cases is provided below.

is heavily dominated by expansive grasslands and wetlands. In February 2026, an unusual

soil moisture and a complete loss of surface storage capacity, the region experienced
catastrophic, out-of-season flooding (https://mapping.emergency.copernicus.eu/news/

2.2. Sentinel-1 SAR Time-Series Dataset
Sentinel-1 SAR observations associated with these flood events were collected from
January 2015 to March 2026. Multiple Sentinel-1 scenes acquired on the same date were
mosaicked and then clipped to the corresponding study-area boundary. All valid Sentinel-1
observations acquired within the corresponding three-month period were retained in the
long-term seasonal observation series, including the target-event images and adjacent same-
season observations. A total of 1184 Sentinel-1 observation times were initially obtained,
including 296 observation times for Guangxi, 276 for Córdoba, 280 for Zagora, and 332
for New South Wales (Table 1). To ensure temporal consistency among observations,
historical Sentinel-1 images acquired during the corresponding seasonal periods of each
flood event were selected for subsequent analysis. Specifically, Sentinel-1 observations
acquired within the same seasonal period during 2015–2026 were retained for constructing
seasonally consistent temporal sequences. The seasonal periods reported in Table 1 refer
specifically to the historical observations used for background time-series construction.
The event-period observations used for TFAM generation were subsequently evaluated
individually against the fitted pixel-wise seasonal distributions. After seasonal filtering,
272 images were selected, including 67 images for Guangxi, 72 for Córdoba, 69 for Zagora,
and 64 for New South Wales.

3. Methods
This study proposes a deep learning-based method for pixel-wise flood classification
and temporal anomaly mapping by integrating long-term Sentinel-1 SAR observations.
The proposed method consists of three steps (Figure 2).
Figure 2. Overview of the proposed method. The ellipsis indicates the intermediate feature layers
(C2–C8), which are omitted for visual clarity.
First, multi-year Sentinel-1 Ground Range Detected (GRD) data are systematically
pre-processed and seasonally filtered to construct a seasonal pixel-based SAR time-series
dataset. Second, a 9-channel feature tensor is constructed from the transformed empirical
distribution, piecewise linear fitting results, and summary statistics of the historical SAR ob-
servations. In parallel, a TCEV model is independently fitted to the historical observations
of each pixel. Third, TabPFN (Tabular Prior-data Fitted Network) uses the extracted histori-
cal SAR statistical features and labeled flood/non-flood samples as context examples for
pixel-wise classification. The resulting classifier is then applied to study area observations
to infer the maximum flood extent at the pixel level. Finally, the inferred maximum flood
extent is integrated with the event-based SAR image and pixel-wise TCEV characteristics to
generate the TFAM, which depicts the spatial distribution of event-induced flood anomalies
relative to historical SAR variability.
3.1. Sentinel-1 Data Processing and Historical Time-Series Construction
To establish a robust historical SAR observation database, Sentinel-1 GRD products
acquired from the Google Earth Engine (GEE; Google LLC., Mountain View, CA, USA)
COPERNICUS/S1_GRD collection were used [43]. These products had already undergone
https://doi.org/10.3390/rs18193323
--- PAGE 8 ---
Remote Sens. 2026, 18, 3323 8 of 32
standard preprocessing in GEE, including precise orbit correction [44], GRD border noise
removal, thermal noise removal [45], radiometric calibration [46], terrain correction [47],
and conversion to decibel (dB) scale [ 48]. The VH polarization was selected because
it is more sensitive for distinguishing flooded and non-flooded surfaces and generally
provides better flood mapping performance than VV polarization [ 49,50]. To minimize
systematic backscatter differences caused by varying observation geometries, Sentinel-
1 images were grouped according to their orbit information before analysis, ensuring
consistent observation conditions across the time series [51].

No additional spatial speckle filtering was applied because such filtering could modify

Permanent water was retained because TFAM characterizes each event observation relative
to the historical distribution of the same pixel. No explicit layover and radar-shadow mask
or additional local incidence-angle correction was applied. The use of consistent orbit
geometry and pixel-wise historical distributions reduces the influence of persistent terrain
and incidence-angle effects but does not completely eliminate them.
Radar backscatter coefficients are influenced by seasonal variations in vegetation
phenology and soil moisture conditions [17], resulting in substantial temporal fluctuations
unrelated to flood events. Directly modeling SAR observations acquired throughout the
entire year may mix these seasonal background variations with flood-induced backscatter
changes, thereby reducing the separability of flood signals from normal environmental
dynamics. Previous SAR time-series flood detection experiments have also reported that
seasonal changes introduced by a longer temporal span can affect detection performance
and that selecting pre-flood observations from the same season can provide more suitable
background information [52]. To reduce broad seasonal differences in land surface condi-
tions, Sentinel-1 observations were filtered according to acquisition months extracted from
image metadata. The acquisition month of each flood event was assigned to one of four sea-
sonal periods (March–May, June–August, September–November, and December–February).
Sentinel-1 observations acquired within the same seasonal period during 2015–2026 were
retained to construct pixel-wise background time series. This procedure represents seasonal
filtering rather than temporal aggregation because the individual observations were not
averaged or composited. It provides a more comparable broad seasonal background but
does not eliminate variations in vegetation, cropping conditions, or soil moisture within
the selected period. These within-period and interannual variations remain represented in
the historical distribution of each pixel and may introduce uncertainty into the detected
anomaly signals. The resulting seasonal SAR time-series stack was resampled to a 30 m
grid using bilinear interpolation and stored in HDF5 format. This grid spacing was selected
to reduce the computational burden of pixel-wise feature construction, TCEV fitting, and
classifier inference across the study areas, at the expense of some spatial detail.

The same-season Sentinel-1 observations used in this study are routine SAR acquisi-
tions rather than block maxima or threshold exceedances. Accordingly, the TCEV model is
used as a two-component parametric approximation of the transformed historical seasonal
distribution at each pixel and is not used for classical return-period estimation. The two
components have a statistical rather than uniquely determined physical interpretation.
One component mainly represents the dominant range of historical backscatter variability,
whereas the second provides additional flexibility for representing the upper tail of the
transformed distribution, which corresponds to unusually low VH backscatter on the
original scale. Such observations may be associated with inundation, although similar
backscatter responses may also result from soil moisture, vegetation, permanent water,
or radar observation geometry. In the proposed framework, this ambiguity is reduced by
restricting TFAM calculation to the TabPFN-derived historical flood extent and interpreting
the resulting conditional anomaly scores together with their spatial context and available
supporting evidence.
The fitted TCEV distribution is independent of the construction of C1–C9. It is subse-
quently used to calculate the cumulative probability of the event-period VH observation at
each pixel. This probability represents the statistical rarity of the observation relative to the
historical seasonal distribution and serves as the anomaly score used for TFAM generation.
Higher probabilities indicate more unusual VH backscatter observations relative to the
historical behavior of the same pixel.
3.3. TabPFN Flood Classification and Temporal Anomaly Mapping
The labeled points used for TabPFN classification were not extracted from the reference
flood extents used for the final accuracy evaluation. Instead, flood and non-flood samples
https://doi.org/10.3390/rs18193323
--- PAGE 11 ---
Remote Sens. 2026, 18, 3323 11 of 32
were manually interpreted and labeled using a high-resolution (3 m) Planet optical image
acquired over Guangxi on 1 October 2025. Pixels showing clear inundation characteris-
tics and clearly non-inundated conditions were selected, while flood boundaries, cloud
shadows, and mixed pixels were avoided where possible. The labels were stored as vector
points, with value = 1 representing non-flood samples and value = 2 representing flood
samples. Before classification, these labels were recoded as y = 0 for non-flood and y = 1
for flood. The nine long-term seasonal SAR statistical features were then extracted at the
labeled locations. Points containing invalid values or missing one or more feature values
were excluded.
A total of 83 valid samples were retained, including 44 flood samples (53.01%) and
39 non-flood samples (46.99%). The nine input variables provided to TabPFN corresponded
to the statistical descriptors C1–C9. Because TabPFN is a pretrained tabular foundation

3.4. Accuracy Assessment of TFAMs
3.4.1. Evaluation of TFAMs
The generated TFAMs were evaluated to quantify their capability in representing
flood-related anomalies and distinguishing flooded areas from non-flooded backgrounds.
Unlike binary flood products, TFAMs provide continuous statistical anomaly scores at the
pixel level, where larger values indicate stronger temporal deviations associated with flood
occurrence. Therefore, the TFAM values were treated as continuous anomaly scores and
compared with the reference flood labels without introducing a predefined threshold.
Before evaluation, a date-specific polygon shapefile was used to define the complete
validation boundary. A common evaluation grid covering this boundary was constructed
using the coordinate reference system, pixel spacing, grid orientation, and pixel alignment
of the corresponding reference raster. The TFAM and reference flood raster were then re-
projected onto this common grid using nearest-neighbor resampling. Within the validation
boundary, reference flood pixels were encoded as 1 and all remaining pixels were encoded
as 0. TFAM values retained their original continuous range of 0–1, whereas pixels without
a TFAM value, including those outside the maximum historical flood extent identified
by TabPFN, were assigned a value of 0. Pixels outside the polygon validation boundary
were excluded.
Consequently, the reference-label array and TFAM-score array contained exactly the
same number of pixels, and all pixels within the date-specific validation boundary were
included in the ROC, precision-recall, threshold-sensitivity, and confusion-matrix calcula-
tions. A reference flood pixel located outside the TabPFN-derived maximum flood extent
was therefore counted as a false negative rather than being excluded, whereas a reference
non-flood pixel in this area was counted as a true negative. The reported metrics thus
represent the end-to-end performance of the complete framework over the reference vali-
dation domain rather than conditional performance restricted to the TabPFN-derived mask.
Resampling the reference products to the common 30 m analysis grid may nevertheless
omit narrow inundated features and alter flood-boundary representation; therefore, the
reported accuracy reflects spatial agreement at the 30 m scale.

3.4.3. Cross-Region Transfer Design and Evaluation Metrics
To further examine cross-region transferability of the TabPFN classification component,
a reciprocal transfer experiment was conducted between Guangxi and New South Wales.
A New South Wales label set was manually interpreted from a high-resolution (3 m) Planet
optical image acquired on 1 April 2022, using the same point-selection criteria applied
in Guangxi. It contained 115 valid samples, including 58 flood samples (50.43%) and
57 non-flood samples (49.57%). The same nine statistical descriptors, C1–C9, were used as
inputs for both regional models.
The Guangxi regional model was directly applied to New South Wales without using
New South Wales labels for refitting. Conversely, the New South Wales regional model
was directly applied to Guangxi without using Guangxi labels for refitting. This design
evaluated reciprocal cross-region model transfer rather than leave-one-event-out validation.
For each target region, the local-model result was used as a comparison baseline
and was not treated as a ground-truth flood label. Prediction results were clipped using
the predefined study-area boundaries for New South Wales and Guangxi, respectively.
Evaluation was performed only over pixels valid in both the local and transferred-model
results. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used to assess consistency between continuous
probability maps. Binary candidate masks generated using the fixed probability thresh-
old of 0.5 were compared using IoU and Cohen’s Kappa to quantify spatial overlap and
classification agreement, respectively. This experiment assessed whether the transferred
model retained the principal probability structure and candidate spatial distribution identi-
fied by the target-region local model; it did not replace independent reference-data-based
flood-mapping accuracy evaluation.

5. Discussion
5.1. Extreme-Event Characterization for SAR Flood Mapping
Flood mapping using SAR observations remains challenging, particularly for rare
and extreme flood events. Existing approaches generally rely on backscatter differences,
predefined thresholds, or data-driven feature extraction, which are effective for iden-
tifying obvious surface changes but may have difficulty distinguishing flood-induced
anomalies from normal temporal variations. This limitation is particularly relevant in
areas with strong seasonal fluctuations, heterogeneous land-cover conditions, and com-
plex background scattering characteristics, where similar SAR responses may arise from
non-flood-related processes.
In this study, TCEV was introduced to characterize flood-related SAR responses from
the perspective of extreme deviations relative to pixel-specific historical behavior. Unlike
conventional statistical methods that mainly describe average temporal variability, extreme
value theory focuses on the tail distribution of observations and is therefore more suitable
for representing rare events. By modeling the probability of extreme SAR responses, TCEV
provides a quantitative description of how unusual an observation is compared with its
historical context. This allows flood detection to move beyond direct comparison of signal
magnitude and toward the identification of statistically abnormal surface responses.
The use of pixel-wise extreme-event characterization also offers a means of reducing
reliance on manually selected thresholds or universally applicable rules. Since different
locations may exhibit distinct background behaviors due to variations in vegetation, soil
moisture, and land-cover conditions, evaluating anomalies relative to local historical dis-
tributions provides a more adaptive interpretation of flood-related changes. Therefore,
incorporating extreme-event information offers a complementary perspective to conven-
tional SAR flood mapping approaches and provides a more transparent interpretation of
flood-related SAR anomalies.
5.2. Integration of Extreme-Value Knowledge and Temporal Deep Learning
Deep learning methods have increasingly been applied to SAR-based flood mapping
because of their ability to learn complex relationships from remote sensing data. However,
many existing approaches primarily treat flood mapping as a classification problem and
depend heavily on the quantity and representativeness of labeled samples. For rare extreme
events, the limited availability of training examples and the differences between historical
conditions and event scenarios may restrict the generalization capability of purely data-
driven models.
The proposed framework combines TabPFN-based classification using nine historical
SAR statistical descriptors with pixel-wise TCEV-based anomaly characterization. Rather
than directly learning from raw SAR observations, the model uses temporal features that
describe pixel-level variability, transition characteristics, and extreme responses. These
features provide information about the historical context of each pixel, while the learning
model identifies relationships between temporal anomaly patterns and flood occurrence.
This framework combines complementary statistical modeling and tabular classi-
fication components. TCEV independently provides a pixel-wise anomaly probability,
indicating whether a SAR response is unusual relative to historical observations. TabPFN
separately integrates multiple statistical descriptors for spatial classification. Together, these
components support the joint characterization of spatial flood candidates and pixel-wise
temporal anomalies under limited labeled-sample conditions.

5.3. Continuous Flood Anomaly Representation Beyond Binary Mapping
Most operational SAR flood products represent flooding as a binary classification
problem, where pixels are assigned as either flooded or non-flooded. Although this repre-
sentation is valuable for rapid assessment and emergency response, it inevitably simplifies
the complexity of flood-related surface changes and does not fully preserve variations in
anomaly intensity.
The proposed TFAM provides a continuous representation of flood-related anomalies
by quantifying the deviation between event-period SAR observations and historical condi-
tions. Unlike binary flood maps that only indicate the presence or absence of inundation,
TFAM characterizes the statistical rarity of event-period VH backscatter relative to the
historical distribution of each pixel. Higher TFAM values indicate more unusual backscatter
observations, rather than greater water depth, flood intensity, or damage severity. Persis-
tent high values across successive acquisitions may suggest sustained inundation when
supported by other flood evidence; however, they do not establish uninterrupted flooding
between observations or directly quantify flood duration. This continuous representation
allows spatial differences in flood responses to be better characterized, particularly in
environments where inundation conditions vary substantially across the landscape.
Another important advantage of TFAM is that anomaly intensity is evaluated accord-
ing to pixel-specific historical behavior rather than absolute SAR values. This reduces
the influence of spatially varying background conditions and provides a more consistent
interpretation of flood-related changes across different environments. Consequently, TFAM
extends SAR flood mapping from simple inundation delineation toward a more informative
characterization of flood anomalies and their temporal variability.
Although the use of consistent orbit geometry and pixel-wise historical distributions
reduces persistent geometric effects, residual speckle, layover, radar shadow, and local
incidence-angle variability may still influence the anomaly scores, particularly in the
mountainous areas of Guangxi and Zagora. Seasonal variations in permanent water and
mixed pixels near water and land-cover boundaries may also produce anomalous SAR
responses unrelated to flooding. In addition, resampling to a 30 m grid reduces the
representation of narrow inundated features and detailed flood boundaries.
The present study did not establish a common transferable threshold for converting
TFAMs into binary flood maps. Reliable evaluation of such a threshold would require
additional independent flood events divided into separate threshold-calibration and testing
datasets. Future work should evaluate common or region-adaptive thresholds using
independent events that are not involved in threshold selection.
Filtering Sentinel-1 observations by orbit and the corresponding three-month period
reduces differences in observation geometry and broad seasonal conditions, although
it cannot ensure that the pixel-wise SAR series is strictly independent and stationary.
Moreover, the limited number of observations may affect the stability of the four-parameter
TCEV fitting. Because this study did not formally compare TCEV with alternative anomaly
models, the current results do not establish the statistical superiority of the two-component
model. Future research should assess parameter stability through resampling and compare
alternative anomaly models using independent events and out-of-sample evaluation.
6. Conclusions
This study proposed a statistical time-series feature classification and pixel-wise TCEV
modeling method for SAR flood anomaly mapping. By integrating pixel-wise extreme-
value modeling, statistical feature construction from multi-temporal SAR observations,
and anomaly-based characterization, the proposed method identifies flood-related SAR
responses based on their deviations from historical variability, providing an interpretable

method for multi-temporal flood monitoring. The main conclusions are summarized
as follows:
(1) The integration of TCEV and TabPFN combines extreme-event characterization with
temporal learning, enabling the identification and temporal characterization of rare
flood-related SAR anomalies from long-term observations.
(2) The proposed TFAM extends conventional binary flood mapping by providing con-
tinuous anomaly representations, allowing more detailed characterization of spatial
heterogeneity and temporal changes in flood responses.
(3) The proposed method demonstrates applicability across diverse flood scenarios and
environmental conditions, with the Guangxi case providing supplementary hydro-
meteorological context rather than direct validation of flood mapping accuracy.
Future studies should further integrate multi-source remote sensing observations
and hydrological information to improve the quantitative interpretation of flood severity.
Moreover, developing scenario-aware adaptive methods that dynamically adjust model
parameters and accuracy benchmarks based on specific flood types and environmental
contexts will be a crucial next step. Extending the proposed method to the continuous, long-
term monitoring of frequently flooded and high-risk regions could enhance operational
disaster response and provide additional insight into historical inundation patterns, thereby
supporting long-term spatial planning and climate resilience strategies.
